Use this guide for **Gods & Kings with Domination victory enabled**. In an ordinary settled game, you must control every major civilization's original capital, including your own. A capital captured and later lost is not currently controlled. Making opponents lose their capitals, or being the only civilization still holding its own original capital, does not substitute for controlling them all. For unusual never-settled or missing-capital cases, follow the targets shown in the victory screen.

## Give the first war a specific purpose

A useful target provides position, resources or a required capital. Compare distance, approach terrain, military technology, allies and your other frontier. The closest neighbor may not be easiest: a city behind a mountain pass can cost more than a somewhat more distant open target.

List what the war should gain and what it will cost afterward. Fighting for a resource available through trade may be poor value if it creates several dangerous enemies. Developing peacefully until a stronger military window is a valid campaign decision.

## Turn technology and production into a concentrated advantage

Select a force you can build or upgrade promptly, then prepare its technologies, resources and upgrade money. A bow-unit upgrade path is useful for practicing sustained fire, but still needs screening, siege support and capture capability. Research alone does not create a modern army.

Your civilization's unique units may change the useful timing. Check the chosen [civilization](/database/civilizations/) and replacement relationships. If its special period has passed, plan around effective current units instead of producing obsolete troops to fulfill a theme.

A military window includes the time needed to reach the front, when the opponent may upgrade and how long you can sustain the offensive. Distance and poor roads can consume a paper technology lead before your army arrives.

## Meet five departure conditions

1. Scout the target and approaches, including tiles where ranged and siege units can deploy.
2. Bring capture-capable melee units, damage against the field army and reserves to replace wounded troops.
3. Budget happiness and gold for the first conquest, with money for essential upgrades.
4. Keep rear defenses and a replenishment plan without stopping every city's development.
5. Set a withdrawal or peace condition: losses or technology changes that make the offensive no longer worthwhile.

A prepared force creates local superiority more reliably than sending newly completed units into combat one at a time. Use [combat and siege tactics](/strategies/combat-siege/) for individual turns.

## Reduce reinforcements before taking the city

Use favorable terrain to draw enemies into concentrated fire. Before assaulting, inspect roads behind the city and likely arriving reinforcements, not just its remaining health.

Preserve a healthy capturer and cover its position for the turn after capture. Repeatedly attacking the city with your only melee unit can leave the entire operation unable to complete its last step.

After taking a capital, inspect victory progress. An opponent's replacement administrative capital is not another original-capital objective. Chasing every relocated capital can add unnecessary campaign costs.

## Consolidation belongs inside the campaign plan

Review resistance, happiness, income, resources and transport. Heal and upgrade the main force before selecting another target. Choose puppeting or annexation according to city purpose and finances; do not automatically annex every conquest in a short burst.

During a pause, let rear cities catch up on science and production buildings. Constant unit production can otherwise leave you behind for the next military generation. A captured position must be defensible for its gains to last.

Accepting a useful peace can prepare another offensive. Whether to eliminate a civilization that has already lost its original capital depends on its remaining threat, geographic obstruction and diplomatic consequences. Two remaining cities are not automatically two required conquests.

## Order targets and prepare overseas operations

| Situation | Planning priority | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One strong and one weak neighbor | Find a campaign that shortens your frontier and accounts for intervention | Choosing only by weakness while exposing the rear |
| A distant rival approaches another victory | Verify its progress and whether you can disrupt the relevant core in time | Spending the available window on irrelevant border cities |
| A capital lies overseas | Prepare navigation, escorts, vision and landing space | Sending an unprotected land army into an enemy navy |
| Another civilization controls a corridor | Evaluate diplomacy, passage or an alternative landing | Assuming a peacetime route remains usable after war begins |

Include travel time in overseas plans. Strong land units can be vulnerable while embarked. Secure sea access and a defensible landing area, with continuing reinforcements after the first wave. Coastal assaults must still respect the capture abilities of the particular naval or land units involved.

## Worked decision: stop after two cities or take a third?

Suppose you hold the target's original capital and a corridor city. Most troops are wounded, happiness has become negative and a third city lies far beyond the line. Continuing extends reinforcement routes without necessarily advancing the victory condition.

Determine whether that city can promptly retake the capital or block your only route. If neither is true, consolidation and a possible peace may better serve the objective. Repair happiness, heal, upgrade and assign roles to the captured cities before moving toward another required original capital. This example does not assume the AI will accept peace.

Before the final push, verify who currently holds every original capital, whether your own is safe, whether an unexplored continent hides a target and whether a city has been recaptured. Maintain rear security until the game confirms victory, while monitoring rivals' science, culture and diplomatic progress.
